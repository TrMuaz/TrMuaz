Lesson 1

Big picture

Arabia before Islam was diverse. It included nomadic Bedouins, settled farmers, merchants, towns and organised kingdoms. Tribal identity was important, but Arabia did not have one ruler or one way of life.

Important: Jāhiliyyah means an age of ignorance of divine guidance. It does not mean that Arabs had no intelligence, knowledge or culture.

Learning objectives

  • Describe tribal and political organisation.
  • Explain social and economic conditions.
  • Compare the main religious groups.
  • Explain the significance of the Hanifs and Kaʿba.
Starter: Why might a tribe be important where there is no strong central government?
A tribe could provide identity, protection, help, justice and access to resources.
Context

Land, people and knowledge

Arabia connected the Mediterranean, Africa, Yemen, Iraq and the Persian world. Its deserts, mountains, oases and coasts produced different ways of life.

🐪

Desert interior

Some communities moved seasonally with camels, sheep and goats to find water and pasture.

🌴

Oases and farms

Settled communities cultivated dates and other crops where water and fertile soil were available.

🏘️

Towns

Makkah, Yathrib and Ṭāʾif developed different economies involving trade, farming and pilgrimage.

🏛️

Kingdoms

Southern and northern Arabia included organised kingdoms influenced by neighbouring empires.

What did people know?

Poetry and memory: Poets preserved tribal history, praised courage and generosity, criticised enemies and expressed love or loss. Genealogies connected people to families and tribes.
Written knowledge: Inscriptions and graffiti show that writing existed across Arabia, although scripts and literacy differed by region. Southern Arabian civilisations left many formal inscriptions.
Survival and trade: People understood stars, seasons, wells, grazing routes, animal care, caravan travel, negotiation and market exchange.

Politics

Tribes and leadership

In much of Arabia, the tribe was the main source of identity and protection. A tribe was made of clans, and clans contained extended families. Members were expected to defend relatives, honour alliances and help settle disputes.

Bedouin means nomadic or semi-nomadic pastoral people—not all Arabs. Survival encouraged courage, patience, hospitality, generosity and loyalty. Raiding and feuds could also create insecurity.

A tribal shaykh usually led through consultation and persuasion, not unlimited power. Wisdom, courage, generosity, wealth and the ability to protect followers strengthened authority.

Protection depended heavily on family and allies. Marriage, treaties and protection agreements linked groups. A person without strong kin support was more vulnerable.

Quraysh in Makkah

Quraysh was a confederation of clans that controlled Makkah. It had no king. Leading families shared responsibilities involving the sanctuary, pilgrims, diplomacy, trade and defence. Quraysh gained influence through custodianship of the Kaʿba and agreements that protected trade routes.

Think: Quraysh had influence, but it did not rule all Arabia as a territorial empire.
Daily life

Society and economy

Social values

Hospitality, courage, generosity, loyalty and keeping promises were praised. However, revenge, tribal warfare and unequal protection could harm vulnerable people.

Makkah trade

Makkah had limited agriculture and depended on exchange. Quraysh organised caravans and made agreements with tribes and neighbouring powers. Routes connected Yemen, Syria, Iraq, East Africa and the Red Sea world.

Pilgrimage and markets

Pilgrims brought demand for food, water, animals and goods. Sacred months and the protected sanctuary encouraged safer gatherings. Markets also supported poetry, negotiation and the sharing of news.

Slavery and vulnerability

Slavery was accepted in society. People could be enslaved through warfare, trade, kidnapping, debt or birth to enslaved parents. Enslaved people lacked the clan protection and freedom enjoyed by free people.

Infanticide: careful interpretation

The Qur’an clearly condemns killing children, including killing from fear of poverty. Classical Islamic teaching also condemns the burial of daughters alive. Modern historians debate how frequent and how specifically female-targeted this was because contemporary evidence is limited.

Balanced conclusion: Islam decisively rejected infanticide, but students should not claim that every tribe or family practised it.

Beliefs

Religious groups

Arabia had a varied religious landscape. Beliefs differed by tribe, town and region.

Monotheism

Who were the Hanifs?

In Islamic understanding, a ḥanīf turns away from idolatry toward pure monotheism. Prophet Ibrahim (AS) is the main model of a ḥanīf. Before Islam, the term is used for people who rejected idols but did not fully join Judaism or Christianity.

They followed

Belief in one God and the religion associated with Prophet Ibrahim (AS).

They rejected

Idol worship and the association of partners with Allah.

They were

Individual seekers of monotheism.

They were not

A large organised church, tribe or political party.

Explain in one sentence: Why are Hanifs important when studying the coming of Islam?
They show that belief in Abrahamic monotheism and rejection of idols existed in Arabia before Prophet Muhammad (SAW) received revelation.
Sacred centre

The significance of the Kaʿba

Before Islam, the Kaʿba was a major sanctuary and pilgrimage centre under Qurayshi custodianship. In Islamic belief, Ibrahim (AS) and Ismāʿīl (AS) raised its foundations and dedicated it to the worship of Allah.

Qur’an connection: Al-Baqarah 2:125 describes the House as a place of return and security. Verse 2:127 describes Ibrahim and Ismāʿīl raising its foundations.

Change and continuity

Islam kept the Kaʿba, pilgrimage and some established rites, but removed idols and redirected worship completely to Allah. The sanctuary therefore shows both continuity of place and transformation of belief.
